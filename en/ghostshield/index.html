GhostShield
A clear coat that doesn't hide the good part

A two-component (2K) clear coating, mixed 2 : 1 by weight. Roll it over timber or polished concrete and you get a deep, mirror-like gloss that leaves the grain and the aggregate underneath completely visible — in fact it pulls more depth out of them than they had before. The cured film is hard, wipes clean and holds no dust, and it seals the surface so water and stains stay on top instead of soaking in.

And here it is on the first screen, before you spend anything: this film ambers in sunlight. That makes it an interior product only — no balconies, no roof decks, nothing outdoors. Read the STRAIGHT TALK section before you decide.

Why GhostShield

Beautiful floors rarely fail structurally. They fail because the top millimetre takes water, stains and shoes every single day — this product's whole job is to stand there and take it instead, while you keep looking at the nice thing underneath.

01

Genuinely clear — it doesn't mask the surface

Most coatings leave a floor slightly cloudy or shifted in tone, and good timber ends up looking like plastic. This one stays clear enough that every grain line, every polished aggregate chip stays perfectly readable. What changes is depth and richness — the way a floor looks when it's wet, except permanently.

02

Deep gloss, not just shine

Rolled on with a foam roller the material flows out into one continuous, level sheet, so the surface reflects an actual image rather than a vague sparkle — which is exactly why cafés, showrooms and galleries reach for a clear coating instead of just polishing the concrete harder.

03

Hard, washable, dust-free

The cured film takes everyday foot traffic and scrubbing, and shrugs off household chemicals and stains · More importantly it has no open pores for stains to sink into. Water, coffee, shoe marks — wipe them up when they happen and nothing permanent is left behind, unlike bare concrete.

04

Stops a concrete floor dusting forever

Test your own slab in three seconds: rub it hard with your palm. If white powder comes off, that floor will keep handing you dust for the rest of its life. · Sealing it with this is cutting the problem off at the source. The dust that used to come back every morning stops there, and the floor still looks like concrete — just glossier.

What should you actually use it on

The question customers ask most is simply "what can I put this on" — so here are the real cases, including which ones are worth it and which ones should send you to a different product.

Case 1 — timber floors indoors

This is where the product pays for itself fastest. Parquet, solid timber, engineered board, matt laminate — the enemy of a wooden floor isn't wear, it's water. A spill nobody wipes up runs into the board joints and swells the edges black, and there is no fixing that short of pulling boards out.

Coating the whole floor closes the joints and seals the surface, so water sits there until somebody deals with it. Meanwhile the grain you paid for is still there, darker and with more depth than before.

Pick the right timber before you commit

Warm mid-to-dark browns suit this best, because the ambering that will eventually happen simply blends into the wood's own colour · White, bleached oak and grey-toned floors will show the shift first. Think about that before ordering.

Rooms with all-day sun are the risky ones

Indoors does not mean out of the sun — the strip of floor in front of a big window or under a skylight will amber before the rest of the room does, and some houses end up with the shape of the window frame printed onto the floor. If that's your room, talk to us first.

Case 2 — polished concrete, cafés, retail, galleries

Polished concrete is a beautiful floor with two large flaws: it dusts, and it drinks stains · One drop of coffee on bare concrete becomes a permanent mark in about ten minutes, which is why a café two years in usually has a floor that reads like a map of everything ever spilled on it.

A clear coat fixes both at once — the dusting stops and the stains stay on the surface — while the floor still looks like polished concrete. The aggregate is still right there, just deeper and glossier. Best suited to spaces that are walked on rather than driven on: coffee shops, clothing stores, showrooms, exhibition rooms, lobbies.

The benefit an owner feels daily

A floor like this closes down far faster at the end of the day, because there are no pores to scrub. One pass with a mop and you're done.

Two more cases that work — with conditions

Case 3 — factory and warehouse floors (indoors only)

It works, and it works very well for stopping dust and keeping oil out of the slab · But be clear about what you're buying: at roughly 200 grams per square metre what you get is a sealing layer, not a thick trowel-applied floor — right for storage areas, walkways, light assembly space and rubber-wheeled trolleys · If you run steel-wheeled forklifts, drag steel pallets, or spill chemicals as a matter of routine, you should be looking at a thick pigmented floor system from the start. Ask us and we'll tell you honestly which one is the better buy.
Case 4 — tables, furniture and resin work

Live edge slabs, single-plank tables, timber counters, shelving and decorative resin castings — this gives a clear glossy surface that makes the grain do all the work and is hard enough to have things put on it every day · The trick is several thin coats rather than one thick one, because furniture gets looked at from thirty centimetres away. Tiny bubbles you'd never notice on a floor are impossible to miss on a tabletop.
Where not to use this — four lines worth remembering

1) Anything outdoors. Balconies, roof decks, external walkways, car parks, fences, signage — it will amber, guaranteed. Use CrystalSeal or clear Polyaspartic instead.
2) Structural waterproofing. A leaking roof deck, a wall wicking damp, a bathroom, a pond, a pool — different category entirely, see Polyurea Gen3 or SiliconePro.
3) Anywhere that must not be slippery when wet. Bathrooms, ramps, shop entrances that catch the rain — a glossy film with water on it is slippery, and we won't sell this one into that role.
4) Floors that are still dusting, cracked or pitted. Fix them first with PatchPro. A clear coat repairs nothing — it just makes everything easier to see.

The gloss is real
but so is the ambering — and we're not hiding it

0outdoor jobs we would recommend this product for

There are products on this site we happily describe as "made for lazy people". This is not one of them. GhostShield gives the best-looking result of any clear coating we sell, and it comes with a short list of conditions attached. We'd rather you learned them now than while the material is going hard in the pot.

The biggest drawback: it ambers

The clear film gradually takes on a yellow cast under sunlight and UV — this is not a bad batch, it is simply how this family of chemistry behaves, all of it · So outdoor work is out entirely, and even indoors the floor in front of a large window or under a skylight will change before the rest of the room does · If your job genuinely cannot tolerate any colour shift, CrystalSeal is the conversation to have instead.

Fifteen minutes is a deadline, not a suggestion

Once mixed you have fifteen minutes to get it on the floor. After that it thickens, and forcing it out anyway leaves ripples you cannot fix · There is exactly one way around this: mix only what you can lay. Break the floor into small bays and mix a fresh cup for each one — nobody has ever lost face for mixing too little, whereas the person who mixed the whole tin at once gets to throw the whole tin away.

Badly mixed means never cures — and never is never

This is the failure we see most often from customers. If A and B are not properly combined, the material simply will not dry. It stays tacky under your fingers all night and the day after, and there is no rescue other than scraping it off and starting again · You must weigh it on a scale, never judge it by eye, and stir for a full minute minimum, deliberately scraping the sides and the bottom of the pot — the material clinging there is the material that never met its other half.

A gloss surface shows everything beneath it

A clear coat is not a repair, it's a magnifying glass — old scratches, patches that didn't colour-match, an uneven slab, dust you didn't vacuum up, all of it becomes more obvious afterwards, not less · The surface must be genuinely dry and as clean as you can get it before you start, and if you want a truly level finish, use a roller, not a brush — a brush leaves its own bristle marks every single time.

Gloss is paid for with grip

The glossier a surface, the more slippery it is with water on it — we will not put the words "anti-slip" anywhere near this product · Interior floors that stay dry are no problem at all, but a shop entrance the rain reaches, or anywhere that gets regular spills, needs a mat in front of it or a different system altogether.

It is not a waterproofing system

This seals the surface so water and stains don't soak into the substrate, which is a completely different job from structural waterproofing · A roof deck that already leaks, a wall drawing damp up from below, a bathroom letting water through to the floor beneath — coating over any of those fixes nothing, and you also risk moisture from underneath pushing the film off in blisters.

How to use it — all of it, on this page

No separate manual to go and find. Every mix ratio and every real timing is right here — read it through before you open the first tin, because after mixing you have fifteen minutes and no time to come back and look something up.

  1. One check first — is this job actually indoors

    If the answer is outdoors, or in direct sun most of the day, stop here and let's talk about CrystalSeal instead · Changing your mind after it's down means grinding the whole floor back, which costs several times what the material did. interior only

  2. Prepare the surface — clean, bone dry, as dust-free as you can manage

    The surface must be genuinely dry all the way through, not just dry on top · Sweep, wash, then vacuum it properly. Every speck of dust left behind gets locked under a clear film permanently, and on a gloss surface you will see all of it · Oil, wax and old polish must be washed off completely. bone dryvacuum, don't sweep

  3. Finish all repairs and abrading first

    Cracks and pits must be filled flush and left flat before you coat — for floor repairs use PatchPro and let it cure fully as stated on that product · Any surface that is already glossy — tiles, previously sealed timber, concrete polished to a shine — must be sanded back to a matt finish first, otherwise there is nothing for the film to key into.

  4. Weigh it: A 2 parts to B 1 part — a scale is mandatory

    Mix ratio is Part A : Part B = 2 : 1 by weight · Real working batches: A 50 g : B 25 g · A 100 g : B 50 g · A 200 g : B 100 g

    Never estimate it by eye and never measure it by volume in a jug — the two parts aren't the same consistency, so measuring by volume throws the ratio off, and an off ratio means a film that never cures. A : B = 2 : 1 by weightdigital scalemix only what you can lay

  5. Stir at least a full minute — sides and bottom included

    Stir continuously for at least one full minute, until the material is uniform with no streaks in it · Scrape the sides and the bottom of the pot into the mix every time, because whatever is stuck there hasn't met its other half yet, and it will end up on your floor as a tacky patch that never dries.

    If the material is too thick to flow properly, you may add up to 10% thinner by weight of the mix — beyond that the film goes thin and loses strength. stir ≥ 1 minutescrape the potthinner ≤ 10%

  6. Apply immediately, finish within 15 minutes

    Get it onto the surface the moment it's mixed · Use a foam roller or a short-nap roller, 4 inch, for the smoothest result · A brush works but leaves bristle marks in a gloss film, so keep the brush for edges and corners only · Work in one direction and don't go back over an area that has already started to set. 4" foam rollerfinish within 15 min

  7. One or two coats, six hours between them

    For most work two coats beat one every time — the first soaks in and satisfies the substrate, the second is the one that builds the gloss · Leave about six hours between coats · For furniture and tabletops that get looked at closely, several thin coats beat one heavy one. 1–2 coats6 h between coats

  8. Waiting times — the real numbers

    Dry to touch 6–12 hours · Light foot traffic after 6 hours or more · Fully cured 24–48 hours

    For the first 48 hours avoid dragging heavy objects, washing it down, or letting water stand on it. A film that hasn't finished curing marks far more easily than people expect · Let furniture reach the full 48 hours before you put anything on it. dry to touch 6–12 hwalk on after 6 hfull cure 24–48 h

  9. Clean tools immediately with thinner

    Wash rollers, brushes and mixing pots while the material is still liquid · Once it sets there is no getting it off again and the tool is finished — don't leave a roller lying around to deal with in the evening.

  10. Storing what's left — seal it tight, keep air out

    Anything already opened must be resealed as tightly as possible and will keep for about 3 months · Humidity in the air is particularly hard on Part B; a lid that isn't properly closed gets you jelly or crystals next time you open it · Store somewhere shaded at room temperature — not in a vehicle and not under a metal roof. ~3 months once opened

The mistakes that waste the most material — never use it outdoors / never mix a whole tin at once, only what you can lay in 15 minutes / never estimate the ratio by eye or measure by volume, weigh it / never leave part of the pot unmixed, that material won't cure and can't be fixed / never coat a surface that is still damp / never add more than 10% thinner
Odour and safety — the material itself doesn't leave the factory with solvent carried in it, so it smells much milder than systems that use thinner as the carrier. Add thinner yourself, of course, and you get the smell of whatever you added · Always work with ventilation, wear gloves, and avoid direct contact with the liquid, Part B especially · Leave a fan running while it cures.
