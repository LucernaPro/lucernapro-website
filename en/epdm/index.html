EPDM Rubber Flooring
Granules, liquid, trowel. Done.

The rubber floor you have seen in playgrounds and running tracks is not a mat someone rolled out. It is coloured EPDM granules mixed with a single-component German-grade polymer binder and trowelled down in place — the trade calls it wet-pour. What you get is one seamless surface that is soft underfoot, absorbs impact, cuts slipperiness, and — this is the part mats can never do — takes any shape you want: curves, circles, around a tree, along a winding garden path. / And it is far less complicated than people assume. A bucket, a drill with a paddle, a steel trowel. No machinery, no specialist crew, no trade certificate.

Why not just buy rubber mats

Most people know rubber flooring as interlocking mats or tiles. Easy to lay, sure. But every one of their problems lives in the same place: the joints.

01

No joints — nothing to trip on, no edges to lift

Mats give you a seam every 50 cm or every metre. Give them a season of sun and rain and the edges start curling, water gets underneath, grit and soil pack into the grooves, and before long you have grass growing out of your playground. / This is laid as one continuous sheet — there is no joint to fail because there was never a joint. And because it is trowelled in place, it follows the real shape of your space: circles, curves, around tree trunks, around posts, right up to a pool edge.

02

Soft, impact-absorbing, and much less slippery

The whole rubber layer works as shock absorption — a child hitting this and a child hitting bare concrete are not the same event. The thicker you lay it, the more it takes. / The fine texture of the granules also cuts slipperiness wet or dry. / Straight talk, house rule: less slippery is not non-slip. Sprint across it soaking wet and you can still go down. Anyone promising you 100% non-slip is selling you something else.

03

Single-component binder — no mixing ratio to get wrong

Our binder is a single-component (1K) German-grade system. Open it and pour. No weighing A against B, no ruined batch because someone eyeballed the hardener. / It cures on humidity in the air (moisture cure), which means there is exactly one thing left for you to control: finish trowelling before your working time runs out. The air handles the rest.

04

Genuinely DIY — no installation crew required

Full tool list: a mixing bucket, a drill with a paddle, a steel trowel, gloves. That is it. No machines, no sprayers, nothing that needs heating or rolling. / The only real skill is getting it flat and evenly thick, and you will learn that on your first bay — which is exactly why you start in the corner nobody looks at.

Where it goes — wider than most people think

Say "rubber flooring" and everyone pictures a playground. It works indoors and out, and the only real condition is that the surface underneath is sound and dry.

STRAIGHT TALK — read this before you pay us

It really is good-looking and easy to lay
and there are 5 things we will tell you first

1KSingle-component binder. Open and pour.

One — it is not a waterproofing system. Water goes straight through it. This is the most important line on the page and the thing people get wrong most often: this floor is an open, permeable structure. The binder glues the granules to each other where they touch, but the gaps between them stay open, and rain runs through to whatever is underneath. / Outdoors that is a feature — nothing puddles. On a roof terrace with a room below, it is a serious problem: you must get a waterproofing system finished first, then lay rubber over the top. Do not expect the rubber to keep water out. It will not.

Two — pale rubber will yellow in the sun. No hedging: outdoors, in white, cream, beige, light grey or pastel, the tone will drift towards a yellow-brown after real sun exposure. It shows up worst where the sun hits hardest, while whatever sits under a tree stays as it was — and then you notice the unevenness. / This is a cosmetic issue, not a structural one. The floor keeps doing its job exactly as before. / The fix: go dark or go saturated outdoors — terracotta, red, green, blue, charcoal, black. On those, the shift is almost impossible to spot. Save the pale colours for indoors, where they last a very long time.

Three — the surface underneath must be sound. Rubber does not fix a bad floor. This is a wearing layer, not a structural one. If the concrete below is cracked, settling, or full of potholes, day one looks perfect and then the base keeps moving and the crack reappears through the surface. / Fix the floor first, always. Cracks and potholes in concrete are a job for PatchPro. / Compacted earth or compacted crushed stone also works as a base, but it has to be genuinely compacted, and you accept a less perfect surface than you get over concrete.

Four — solid colour through the full thickness costs more than it needs to. Coloured EPDM granules cost several times what black granules cost. Lay 10 mm of solid colour and you are paying the colour premium on 5–6 mm nobody will ever see. / What people who do this for a living actually do: lay a black base layer first, then close it off with a thin colour layer on top. Same thickness, same softness, visually identical — and materially cheaper. The two-layer method is written out step by step below.

Five — once the binder cures, cleaning tools is close to hopeless. Brace yourself: cured binder does not really come off a trowel or a bucket. / The practical answer is use old tools, or cheap tools you are willing to throw away, and clean with thinner immediately while everything is still wet. Not tomorrow. / Mix in a cheap plastic bucket. It costs less than the hour you would spend scraping a good one.

How thick should you go

Simple rule: the further someone can fall, the thicker it goes. / Paths, balconies, courtyards, indoor floors — thin is fine, you are buying the surface and the grip. / Gyms — you are buying impact absorption from equipment. / Playgrounds with tall equipment — the thickest of the lot. / Tell us what the space is and how high the equipment stands and we will tell you the thickness, so you are not guessing and then coming up short mid-job.

Colours mix — you are not stuck with one

Coloured granules can be blended before the binder goes in. Two-tone speckle, three-tone, stripes, a different-coloured circle in the middle of the court — all fine, because this is trowelled in place. / Technique for patterns: lay the darker colour first, let it set, then come back and butt the lighter colour up against it. That gives you a clean line instead of two colours bleeding into each other.

Keep the pail shut

Specific to single-component binder: it cures on moisture in the air, which means the air in your open pail is also curing it. / Opened it and did not use it all? Reseal it as tightly as you can, right away, and store it somewhere dry and cool. Never leave it open overnight.

Looking after it

Easy — sweep off leaves and grit now and then, hose it down as normal. / What to watch is sharp things and heavy things dragged across it: steel table legs, narrow steel trolley wheels. Those bite in. / Damage stays local and repairs locally — cut out the damaged patch, mix a fresh batch, trowel it in, and it bonds to what is already there.

Working safely

Wear gloves, work with air moving, and don't spend the day breathing the vapour — indoors especially, open it up or put a fan on it. / Once the floor has fully cured it is safe, and behaves like any other floor: kids on it, pets on it, no issue. But while you are working, treat it as chemical work, not as painting.

How to use it

Every number is on this page. There are only three things that actually decide whether this job goes well: the base is sound and dry, every granule genuinely gets coated, and you only mix what you can trowel in 30 minutes.

  1. Prepare the base — sound, clean, and bone dry

    Concrete, tile, asphalt, or well-compacted crushed stone all work, as long as the base carries the load and does not move. / Fix cracks and potholes first, always (on concrete, that is PatchPro). / Sweep off dust, soil, oil stains and moss — dust is a release layer between the binder and your floor. / The base must be dry, with no standing water and no trapped damp. Do not shortcut this one; the reason is in the warning box below.

    Base must not moveRepair cracks firstBone dry only

  2. Decide now: one layer or two

    Single layer (solid colour, full thickness): simplest, done in a day, right for thin work and smaller areas. / Two layers (black base, colour top): lay black granules as the base, then close it off with a thin colour layer — same thickness, same softness, visually identical, and a lot cheaper, because coloured granules cost several times what black ones do. / For playgrounds and large areas we recommend two layers every time.

    Thick job = two layersBlack under, colour over

  3. Roll a thin coat of binder onto the base, then lay while it is tacky

    Before any rubber goes down, put a thin coat of binder onto the base with a roller or brush. It improves how the layer grips, and it matters most on power-trowelled concrete, which is slick. / Lay the rubber while that coat is still tacky — do not let it dry off completely first.

    Prime with binderLay while tacky

  4. Mix — granules in first, binder on top, then 3–5 minutes

    Weigh the granules into the bucket, then pour the binder over them at 1 part binder to 6 parts granules by weight. / Mix with a drill and paddle for 3–5 minutes, until every granule has the same sheen and there are no dull ones left — a dull granule is an uncoated granule, and that is the one that comes loose later. / Only ever mix what you can trowel out in 30 minutes. Mix one enormous batch and fall behind, and you throw the whole bucket away.

    1 : 6 by weightMix 3–5 minutesEvery granule must shine

  5. Tip it out and trowel it down

    Tip the batch onto the floor, then spread and compact it with a steel trowel to the thickness you want. Setting a screed rail or a string line for depth first saves a lot of grief. / The trowel will drag and pick up granules — deal with that by spraying Sonax onto the face of the trowel now and then and it slides again straight away (that is trowel lubrication only, it never goes into the mix). / Compact evenly: under-compact and granules shed underfoot later; over-compact and you have squeezed out the softness you paid for.

    Steel trowelSonax on the trowel faceCompact evenly

  6. Two-layer jobs — colour goes on once the black has set

    Let the black layer set for 12–24 hours, until you can walk on it without sinking in or sticking to it. / Then roll another thin coat of binder over the black and lay the colour layer while that is still tacky, trowelling it the same way. / The colour layer can be much thinner than the base, because its job is appearance and feel — the impact work is being done by the black underneath it.

    Black sets 12–24 hBinder between layers

  7. Let it cure — walk on it at 24 h, use it properly at 48–72 h

    Keep everyone off it and keep water off it while it cures. / Light foot traffic after 24 hours. / Full use — running, playing, equipment set down on it — after 48–72 hours. / Cold or very humid weather is slower, hot weather faster. When in doubt, give it another day. Nobody has ever been hurt by waiting.

    Walk on at 24 hFull use 48–72 hNo water while curing

  8. Clean tools with thinner the moment you stop

    Trowel, bucket, paddle — thinner, immediately, while the binder is still wet. / Being straight with you: it is hard work even then. What people who do this for a living choose instead is old tools, or cheap ones they are happy to bin. Nobody brings their favourite trowel to this job. / Mix in a cheap plastic bucket and let it go.

    Thinner, immediatelyUse old tools
⚠ Never work on a wet base or with rain coming: this binder cures on moisture — the right amount of humidity in the air is exactly what it wants. But standing water, or rain landing on it before it sets, causes bubbling inside the layer, a blistered porous surface, and failed adhesion, and the only fix is to take it up and do it again. / Check the forecast before you start outdoors: you want 6–8 clear hours ahead of you.
⚠ Never mix more than you can trowel out in 30 minutes: a batch that has started to set will not spread, will not compact, and finishes as loose crumbs. Bin it.
⚠ Never add thinner to the mix to stretch it: diluted binder means a thinner film holding the granules together, which means granules shedding in the first year. You save money today and relay the whole floor later. (Sonax is for lubricating the trowel face only — it never goes into the mix.)
⚠ Reseal the binder pail tightly every time: humidity in the air will cure it inside the pail. Finished for the day, close it hard, store it dry and cool. Never leave it open overnight.
⚠ Gloves on, air moving: avoid direct skin contact and prolonged breathing of the vapour, and always ventilate indoor work — once the floor has fully cured it is safe and children can play on it as normal.
Temperature and timing: work above 10°C on a dry base. / Outdoors, start in the morning so the floor has a full day to cure before the night humidity arrives, and avoid trowelling onto a surface baking in direct midday sun — because the hotter it is, the shorter your working time gets. On a 35°C site, plan for about 20 minutes a batch, not 30.
