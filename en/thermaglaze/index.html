We sell floor paint, and we are still not going to tell you it fixes everything — because almost every disappointed customer we have ever had was let down not by the coating but by the floor underneath it. Read these five points and you will know for yourself whether your job is ready to buy or not yet.

A dusting floor means the film bonds to dust, not to concrete

Simple test: rub the floor hard with the flat of your hand. If fine white powder comes away, the surface layer of that concrete has already broken down. Coat over it and the film bonds beautifully to that powder — and the powder is not bonded to anything. It looks great for a while and then lifts in sheets. Said plainly: a tired, dusting concrete floor really will shed a coating, and no brand on the market changes that. The fix is in the instructions below — abrade back to sound concrete and prime with Marine Guard first.

The smell is strong, and we are not going to be coy about it

This is a solvent-borne product. It smells strong — far stronger than a water-based house paint. A sealed room you cannot ventilate is a no. Keep air moving for the whole job and while it dries, wear an organic-vapour respirator rather than a dust mask, and keep every source of flame and spark out of the area. If there are children or elderly relatives in the house, pick a day they are out. The good news is that once the smell goes, it is gone for good.

The finish follows the floor you already have

Floor paint is not a levelling compound. A wavy floor stays wavy, pinholes stay visible, old trowel marks stay where they are. The mirror-flat floors in the photographs are a poured self-levelling system, which is a different job entirely. If you want flat, you have to fix the floor, not the paint.

Glazed tile and polished concrete are not its job

On glazed ceramic tile and power-trowelled polished concrete this will not hold. Not "it will look slightly disappointing" — it will not bond in the first place. Don't spend money finding that out. Tiled floors have a product built specifically for them → TileCoat Polyurea

Solvent re-dissolves the film — which cuts both ways

A thermoplastic film dries by solvent evaporation, so it stays soluble in its own solvent. The upside is that repair and maintenance are genuinely easy: coat a worn patch directly with no abrading, and the new material fuses into the old film instead of sitting on it, so there is no visible lap. The downside is that spilled thinner, fuel or strong solvent left sitting will damage the film — a workshop that sees that regularly should be looking at a cross-linked two-component system instead → Epoxy-PU floor coating

Pigmented or clear — how to choose

Same system, identical application, the only difference is pigment. Find the line that matches your floor.

The floor is stained, patchy and uneven in colour and you want it to end up one colour throughout→ Pigmented
Garage, carport, parking bay — tyre marks and oil and you would rather not see them→ Pigmented — dark grey or black
Sound broom-finished concrete or terrazzo that you actually like, you just want it fresher and no longer dusting→ Clear
A deliberately bare concrete look you want to keep, rather than a painted floor→ Clear
Clear does not mean invisible — the surface will read slightly darker and richer than it did before, the way concrete looks when it is wet. That is normal for every clear coating system, not a defect. If you want to see it before you commit, splash some water on the floor: the colour you see while it is wet is roughly the colour you will get.

How to use it — all of it, on this page

No separate manual to go and find. The real numbers are all here.

  1. Test the floor first, every time

    Rub the floor hard with the flat of your hand — white powder on your palm means the surface layer has broken down and must be dealt with first. This step is not optional. Freshly poured concrete needs a minimum of 28 days to cure before coating; moisture still working its way out will blister the film. hand-rub dust testnew concrete: 28 days

  2. Finish all crack and hole repairs first

    Floor paint does not bridge cracks. Coat over one and it is still there, and it will come back through — fill it properly and level it flush first. For floor repairs use PatchPro and let it cure as stated on that product.

  3. Abrade and clean

    Grind or abrade the friable surface back to sound concrete, take off any old coating that is lifting, then remove every trace of oil, grease and dust and vacuum properly. The floor must be bone dry before you coat. A solvent-borne film over trapped moisture blisters. abrade to sound concreteno oilbone dry

  4. Dusting or very thirsty floor → prime first

    Not every job needs a primer — only floors that still dust after abrading, or concrete so porous it drinks the coating. Prime those with Marine Guard to bind the surface and kill the absorption, then coat ThermaGlaze over it. Sound floor, skip this step.

  5. Ventilate and put your protection on before you open the pack

    Doors and windows open, a fan helps · organic-vapour respirator, gloves, eye protection · no flame, no sparks, no hot work in the area · never work alone in an enclosed space. ventilate throughoutno ignition sources

  6. Stir it properly — and add nothing

    Pigment settles in storage. Stir from the bottom of the pack until colour and consistency are uniform, not two swirls across the top · Nothing goes in: open it and use it · Do not thin it to stretch coverage — a thinner film throws away the durability you paid for. 1K, no mixingnever thin to stretch it

  7. First coat — as thin as you can physically make it

    Short-nap roller, brush or spray · On a thirsty floor, make the first coat as thin as you possibly can — its job is not to look good, it is to seal the surface and stop the absorption. Once the surface is sealed, the second coat sits on the floor instead of disappearing into it.

    This is where most material gets wasted. On an absorbent floor the first coat is supposed to look bad — thin, patchy, concrete showing through. That is normal on every job. Anyone meeting this system for the first time tends to feel it isn't enough and keeps going back over the same patch chasing full colour on coat one — and the floor will happily keep drinking it. Several kilos disappear and the floor looks no better for it, and the person paying for those extra kilos is the customer. Hold your nerve and wait for coat two. That is where the finish comes from.

    Split the area into bays and decide your working direction before you start, so you are never walking back across wet coating. coat 1 = sealing coatdon't re-work the same patchplan direction first

  8. Wait 2–4 hours, then coat two, crosswise

    Touch dry in roughly 30–60 minutes, but leave 2–4 hours before the second coat (longer in cool or humid weather — go by touch, not by the clock). Lay the second coat across the direction of the first so you are not left with thin lines where the roller overlapped. Two coats is standard; a very absorbent floor may want three. touch dry 30–60 minrecoat 2–4 hcoat 2 crosswise

  9. Clean tools with thinner, immediately

    Solvent-borne — clean with thinner while the coating is still wet. Water will not touch it, and once it has dried it is a genuinely miserable job. Don't park the roller and deal with it in the evening.

  10. Foot traffic at 24 hours · full service at 7 days

    Light foot traffic after 24 hours · keep heavy loads, dragging, abrasion and standing water off it for the first 7 days while the remaining solvent leaves the film and it reaches full hardness · vehicles should wait the full 7 days as well. walk on it: 24 hfull service: 7 days

The mistakes that waste the most product — Never coat glazed ceramic tile or polished concrete, it will not bond / Never coat a damp floor, or one with moisture rising from below, the film will blister — if there is permanent moisture coming up through the slab, that has to be solved first / Don't coat outdoors with rain coming / Don't thin it to stretch coverage / and don't use it in a sealed room you cannot ventilate. That last one is a safety rule, not a quality one.

Where it pays for itself

Bare concrete isn't just untidy — it dusts continuously, absorbs everything spilled on it, and never comes properly clean. The place this earns its money is the floor you find yourself sweeping and mopping every week.

An old stained floor — done in two coats

The jobs where the difference is most obvious are floors covered in patchy staining that no amount of washing will shift, because those stains soaked into the concrete long ago and scrubbing only reaches the top of them. A pigmented coat closes the whole argument and hands back a floor that is one colour again.

More useful than the appearance: from now on, whatever gets spilled sits on the film rather than soaking into the concrete — so it wipes off, instead of becoming next year's permanent stain.

The floor stops dusting

Bare concrete keeps shedding fine powder for its entire life — sweep today and it is back tomorrow. Sealing the surface shuts off the source of that dust. Storerooms, garages and workshops with machinery in them often get more out of this than out of the way it looks.

Recoat any time, without stripping anything

The day it has worn enough that you want it fresh again: clean it, let it dry, and coat straight over the top — no stripping, no sanding, because the new material fuses into the existing film as one layer. On a floor that gets maintained every few years, that saves more in labour than most people expect.
