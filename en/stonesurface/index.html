Stone Coating
Self-Cleaning System for Stone and Concrete

Granite, marble, GRC, fair-faced concrete, stone balustrades, sculptures — porous materials that soak up water and then go "black, mouldy, chalky" from the inside. This is a two-layer system: a deep-penetrating primer that stops water getting in plus a self-cleaning topcoat that lets sun and rain keep the outside clean. Both layers are clear; the stone's colour and texture stay exactly as they are.

Why stone needs two layers of protection

Stone doesn't just get dirty on the surface — water soaks into the pores and carries the dirt in with it, so the problem starts inside and outside at the same time.

01

Inside — a deep-penetrating primer that closes the pores

A silicone-family primer soaks into the pores of stone and concrete so the material stops absorbing water, oil and stains — cutting off the source of black mould, algae and efflorescence that surface from within. No film on the surface: the stone still breathes, "vapour out, water not in".

02

Outside — a self-cleaning film

A superhydrophilic surface with a water contact angle under 15°: rain spreads into a thin sheet, gets under the dirt and carries it off, leaving no beads and no water rings. Plus nano titanium dioxide that sunlight activates to keep breaking down oil, soot and mould on the surface.

03

Clear — the stone looks like stone

Neither layer adds colour or gloss. Matt stone stays matt, polished stays polished, the natural veining and colour stay as they are — unlike stone glazes that leave the surface looking like plastic.

04

Fewer of the expensive, risky stone washes

Building facades, bridge balustrades, sculptures, plazas — none of them can be pressure-washed or acid-washed over and over without eating the surface. The system's developer quotes 3–5 years of protection from a single application.

Layer 1 · Penetrating primer

A silicone-family agent that soaks into the pores and forms a clear barrier below the surface — against water, oil and seeping stains, the root cause of black mould, algae, efflorescence and water erosion.

Layer 2 · Self-cleaning topcoat

A surface film that does two jobs — superhydrophilic so rain washes, photocatalytic so sunlight breaks grime down. Applied over the primer once the primer has set.

Surfaces it suits

Granite, marble, sandstone, GRC, fair-faced concrete, render, exposed brick, stone sculptures, balustrades, stone paving — any porous material that absorbs water.

Why prime first

Put a topcoat on stone that still absorbs water and moisture will push from inside and efflorescence will come through anyway — the primer cuts the problem at source, the topcoat keeps the face clean.

Project work — the standard 6 steps

The same procedure the developer uses on GRC and granite facades in China — "waterproof from inside, self-cleaning from outside"

  1. Site survey and feasibility check

    Identify the stone type, porosity, degree of weathering, existing staining and the environment, then plan the job and site access.

  2. Test patch before the full job

    Apply both layers to a small corner of the project's real stone to confirm adhesion, water repellency, the self-cleaning effect and — most importantly — that the stone's colour and texture don't change.

  3. Clean and prepare the surface

    Use a dedicated stone cleaner and suitable tools to remove dust, stains, algae and old sealers completely, then let the stone dry fully — a primer cannot penetrate wet stone.

  4. Two layers — primer first, then topcoat

    Apply the penetrating primer evenly until the stone is saturated, let it soak in and set, then apply the self-cleaning topcoat over it in a thin, even layer.

  5. Natural cure, about 4 hours

    Leave the system to set in air until it forms two clear protective layers, above and below the stone surface.

  6. Inspect and hand over

    Test water repellency, stain resistance and the self-cleaning effect on site, and give the owner a summary before closing the job.

How to apply — everything on this page

Read to the end before opening the bottle — stone work has two failure points: the stone must be dry before priming, and you must test for colour change every time.

  1. Always test a small patch first

    Some stones (especially dark polished stone and some sandstones) may darken slightly after priming. Do a small patch in an unseen corner, let it dry, and compare before treating the whole area.

  2. Wash the stone clean, then let it dry completely

    Remove algae, black mould, oil and old wax completely. After washing the stone must be truly dry — thick stone may need overnight. The primer can only enter pores that are free of water.

    Stone must be fully dry

  3. Layer 1 — prime until the stone is saturated

    Apply by brush, roller or low-pressure spray until the surface is "full". Very porous stone may need a second wet-on-wet pass. Wipe off any excess before it sets so it doesn't leave a glossy residue, then let it soak in and set.

    Brush / roller / low-pressure sprayWipe excess before it sets

  4. Layer 2 — a thin self-cleaning topcoat

    Apply over the primer with a fine spray or a short-nap roller in a thin, even coat. Never apply thick — on dark polished stone an over-thick coat can show a rainbow sheen.

    Thin coat only

  5. Keep water off until cured, ~4 hours

    Water on the topcoat before it cures leaves marks. Always check the rain forecast for outdoor work. Once cured it can take rain; wash with plain water or a neutral cleaner only.

    Cure ~4 h
⚠ Safety: Both products contain solvent and are flammable — work in a ventilated area away from sparks and heat; wear gloves, goggles and an organic-vapour mask when spraying. Keep away from children and direct sunlight.
STRAIGHT TALK — what needs saying

Before you pay, read these 6 points —
this is not a miracle product, and we don't want you to misunderstand it

6things most sellers won't tell you
1 · Some stone darkens slightly after priming

In principle a penetrating primer "doesn't change colour", but dark polished stone and some sandstones may darken a little or look richer — we don't guarantee zero change on every stone. Always test a small patch first. If you can't accept that, don't buy.

2 · It doesn't remove stains that are already there

This system protects, it doesn't clean — black mould, rust stains and efflorescence already in the stone must be washed out first. Apply over old stains and they stay, and the primer won't penetrate.

3 · Water and sunlight are the final carriers

Stone under cover that rain never reaches and sun never hits gets mainly the primer's benefits (water and mould resistance); the "cleans itself" effect is far weaker than outdoors. Don't expect the same result.

4 · Wet stone = a failed job

The primer can only enter dry pores. Apply it to stone still damp from washing or rain and it sits on the surface as a glossy residue and never goes in — the number-one cause of failed jobs with every penetrating system on the market.

5 · The cure window is the danger zone

Water on the topcoat before it cures = marks that need washing off and redoing. Always check the forecast before outdoor work.

6 · Not a once-for-life treatment

Protection lasts around 3–5 years depending on sun, rain and surface wear, then the effect fades gradually, not all at once — reapply on the same stone when it's due, and in between never acid-wash or pressure-wash at close range.
