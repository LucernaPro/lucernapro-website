Why it has to be something you pour

Most people repair a floor crack by forcing mortar into it, and then run into the same three problems every single time — it never reaches the bottom, it never grips the sides, and it splits again along the same line.

01

Poured, not packed — so the crack actually fills

A crack in a floor is not a neat rectangular channel. It wanders, pinches shut in the middle, and opens out again underneath. / Pushing stiff mortar in with a trowel therefore seals the mouth and nothing else — the void underneath is still a void, and it collapses the first time real weight goes over it. / This material is self-flowing: pour it at the mouth and it works its way down to the bottom and into the undercuts by itself, along the whole run.

02

Flexible — so the floor can move without splitting the repair

The reason repaired floors crack again in the same place is almost never that the filler was too weak. It is that it was too rigid to move with the slab. / Outdoor concrete expands and contracts every single day. Put something hard and brittle into a joint that is already moving and the stress simply goes back to the same point and opens it again. / This material keeps a degree of flexibility, so it absorbs that movement instead of fracturing. That is the real product, not the strength number.

03

No weighing, no recipe to get wrong

The ratio is already set inside the box — pour the whole liquid container into the whole powder bag and mix. That's the instruction. / There is no "add a bit more water to loosen it up", no A-to-B ratio, no scales. / The quiet benefit of this is that every box comes out the same, no matter who on site happens to be mixing that day.

04

Works outdoors, handles standing water

Sun and rain are fine, and it copes well with water sitting on top of the repair — car parks that pond after a downpour, rooftops that never drain quite right. / But let's be precise: this repairs cracks, it is not a waterproofing system. Water stops going down that crack, certainly. That is not the same as the floor being waterproof. Different job, different product.

What it's right for — and what it's wrong for

This is the most important section on the page, so please read it before ordering. This product does one thing and does it very well — used on the wrong job the result is poor, and we would rather not take your money for that.

✓

Right — running cracks, roughly 1 cm wide and up

This is the whole point: the crack has to be wide enough for the material to flow down into it. From about 1 cm upwards is where it performs best. / Long splits across a car park, cracks running out from columns, a line travelling the length of a factory floor, slab joints that have opened up — that is its work.

✕

Wrong — potholes and broad broken-out areas

No hedging on this one: do not use it on potholes. It is designed to be poured into a narrow channel, not spread as a wide sheet — poured across a broad area, the surface can blister, and then it has to be chipped out and done again. / Potholes and patch repairs belong to PatchPro, which was built for exactly that.

✕

Wrong — floors you intend to paint or coat afterwards

If the plan is repair the cracks, then paint the floor or lay a waterproofing system over the top, the product you want from the start is PatchPro, not this one. / CreteRevive is for floors that get repaired and then left as they are — bare power-floated concrete, car parks, driveways, factory floors with nothing going over them.

✕

Wrong — hairline cracks, and anything on a wall

Hairline cracks (too fine to slide paper into) can't take a poured material because there is nowhere for it to go — the fix is to cut the crack open first, described in the how-to below. / And walls are simply not its job: this is a flowable material that depends on gravity, so on a vertical surface it runs. For walls, use FillerAce.

One — once it's mixed, it all has to be used. There is no saving it. It can't be kept until tomorrow, and it can't be loosened back up with water (adding water only makes it permanently weaker). / So finish preparing every metre of crack first, and mix last. Mixing and then wandering off to scrape out the channel is the single most common way this job gets ruined.

Two — potholes. Please don't. We said it above but we're saying it again because we see it constantly: people notice how easily it pours and decide to pour it into a wide hole. / The result is a blistered surface that has to be broken out and redone — product wasted, day wasted. / The test is this simple: if it's a line, use this. If it's a hole or a patch, use PatchPro.

Three — the channel has to be properly clean, not just look clean. An old crack is packed with concrete dust, loose grit, sand and oil laid down over several years. / Pour on top of that and what the repair is bonded to is dust, not concrete — and one day the whole run lifts out in a single neat strip. / Blow it out, vacuum it, or wash it and let it go damp-dry. This step takes longer than the pouring does, and it is the step that decides whether the repair lasts.

Four — flexible does not mean it stretches like rubber. We wrote "a degree of flexibility" and the qualifier is deliberate. / It comfortably absorbs the daily thermal movement of a slab, which is behind about nine out of ten repeat cracks. / But if your floor is actually subsiding — ground settling underneath, a foundation problem, the crack visibly wider every month — no crack repair product on earth is going to save it. That is a structural problem. Fix the cause, then come back and repair the surface.

Five — you will still be able to see where the repair is. Don't expect it to disappear: new material and concrete that has been weathering for years will never be the same colour, and the texture won't match either. / The standard for this job is "the crack is gone and it stays gone", not "you can't tell it was ever repaired". Anyone promising you the second one is selling hope. / If you genuinely need one uniform surface across the whole slab, the only route is to repair it properly and then paint the entire floor — and if that's the plan, go back and read the part where we told you to use PatchPro from the beginning.

What if the crack is very deep

If the channel is alarmingly deep, don't fill the entire depth with repair material — it's wasteful and it doesn't make the repair any stronger. / What we do is pour a little dry sand into the bottom of the channel first, leaving a sensible depth at the top for the repair material, then pour on top of that. / The sand is nothing more than a filler taking up volume; the bonding and the load-carrying are done by the material above it.

What "mix it properly" actually means

Not three lazy stirs. Mix until there are no lumps of powder left and no dry powder stuck to the bottom or the sides of the bucket. / Scrape the bottom and the walls of the bucket back into the mix with a trowel, every time. The corner where the base meets the wall is where people always miss, and that is precisely where dry powder collects. / Unevenly mixed material sets to different strengths along the run, and the run fails at its weakest point.

Hot weather shortens your working time

Same as anything that sets by itself: the hotter it is, the faster it goes. / Pouring in full afternoon sun and pouring at eight in the morning are two different jobs — outdoor work should be done early or late, both for the longer working time and to stop the surface drying out too fast and crazing.

How many passes does it need

Normally one — fill it flush and leave it. / But if the level drops because the material has run away into a void underneath (which does happen with old cracks), just top it up straight away while it's still wet, or let it set and pour a second time. New material bonds to the first pour perfectly well. / Walk the whole run once more before you pack up. Always.

Cleaning your tools

Plain water, immediately, while it's still wet — bucket, mixing paddle, trowel. It takes under a minute at that stage. / Let it harden in the bucket and your only option is a chisel; no solvent will help you, because once it's set it doesn't dissolve back. / Same trick as always in this house: use a plastic bucket that costs pocket change. Far better than chiselling out a good one.

How to use it

Every number is on this page — and the whole job comes down to three things: the channel must be wide enough and genuinely clean, mix it properly, including the bottom of the bucket, and whatever you mix, pour it all in one go.

  1. First decide whether this is even the right product

    Walk the length of the crack and ask two questions: (1) is it a line or a hole? — if it's a hole or a broken-out area, stop here and use PatchPro / (2) are you painting or coating this floor afterwards? — if yes, again PatchPro. / Clear on both, carry on.

    line = yeshole = nocoating over it = no

  2. Open the crack to at least about 1 cm wide

    Already wide enough? Skip this step. / For hairline or narrow cracks, run an angle grinder with a masonry disc along the line of the crack and open it to roughly 1–1.5 cm wide, with a depth at least equal to the width. / Cut it with straight sides rather than as a V — straight sides give you far more bonding area on both walls. / It sounds like extra work, but skipping it produces a poor result every time, because the material has no route to the inside of the crack.

    angle grinder, masonry disc1–1.5 cm widedepth ≥ width

  3. Clean the channel out completely

    Knock away every loose piece of concrete along the edges first — if it keeps breaking away, you haven't reached sound material yet, so keep going. / Then blow out or vacuum the bottom of the channel; wash it out if it's badly contaminated. / Any engine oil has to be scrubbed off — oil is the number one enemy of adhesion, and no repair product bonds through it. / Before pouring, the channel should be damp-dry or dry, with no water sitting in the bottom.

    remove all loose materialblow the dust outno standing water

  4. Very deep channel — bed it out with sand first

    If the channel is deeper than it needs to be, pour some dry sand into the bottom and leave a sensible depth at the top for the repair material. / Level the sand off evenly along the whole run — no high and low spots, or the repair ends up at inconsistent depths and doesn't set uniformly. / This is only for unusually deep channels; ordinary cracks don't need it.

    dry sand onlylevel it offdeep channels only

  5. Get everything ready before you touch the box

    This isn't advice, it's a rule: the clock starts the moment you mix and it doesn't stop. / Before mixing, make sure every metre of channel is prepared · the bucket and paddle are in your hand · the water for cleaning tools is already standing by. / If the run is too long to pour within 30 minutes, break it into sections and mix one box at a time — don't mix everything at once.

    prepare the channel firstlong runs = work in sections

  6. Mix — whole container into whole bag, then mix it properly

    Nothing to measure. The ratio is already set in the box — pour the entire liquid container into the entire powder bag (liquid in first, then the powder, is easier to mix). / Mix for about 2–3 minutes, with a drill and paddle or by hand. / You've mixed enough when: it's a single smooth cream, there are no lumps of powder, and there is no dry powder left on the bottom or sides of the bucket — always scrape the base and walls back into the mix with a trowel. That is where people go wrong most often. / Never add water to loosen it.

    nothing to measuremix 2–3 minutesscrape the bucketno added water

  7. Pour it in — fill it and strike it flush

    Pour slowly at the mouth, following the line of the crack, and let the material find its way down. Don't dump it in all at once and have it spill out across the floor. / Fill it flush with the surrounding slab, then strike the surface off neatly with a small trowel and wipe up anything outside the channel straight away, while it's wet — once it's hard it has to be scraped. / If the level drops because there's a void underneath, top it up there and then. Walk the whole run once more before you finish.

    pour slowly, let it flowstrike it flushtop up if it drops

  8. Let it cure — 4–6 h to walk on · 24 h normal use · 7 days for vehicles

    Light foot traffic after 4–6 hours. / Normal use (walking, putting things down, washing the floor) after 24 hours. / Vehicles driving over it, heavy loads sitting on it, or permanent standing water — wait 7 days, because the material needs the full cure time. / Cold or very humid conditions are slower, hot conditions faster. If you're not sure, give it another day. Nobody has ever regretted waiting. / Wash your tools with plain water the moment you stop — let it harden in the bucket and it's a chisel job.

    walk on: 4–6 hnormal use: 24 hvehicles: 7 dayswash tools immediately
⚠ Do not use it on potholes or broad broken-out areas: it is designed to be poured into a narrow channel — spread as a wide sheet, the surface can blister and the whole thing has to be chipped out and redone / potholes and patch repairs go to PatchPro
⚠ Never add water to loosen the mix: the ratio in the box is the point at which the material is strongest — adding water means permanently weaker material, more shrinkage, and cracks appearing in the repair itself within months. You save an afternoon and buy yourself the whole job again
⚠ Once mixed, it all has to be used: it can't be kept until tomorrow and it can't be stirred back to life — prepare the channel first, mix second, not the other way round
⚠ Never pour into a channel with standing water or oil in it: water dilutes the material locally, and oil means no bond at all — clean it and let it go damp-dry first
⚠ Wear gloves and eye protection: the mixed material is alkaline and will attack skin on prolonged contact, and any splash in the eye needs flushing with plenty of clean water immediately — treat this as a construction chemical, not as paint
Temperature and when to work: like anything that sets by itself — the hotter it is, the faster it sets and the less working time you have. / Outdoor work should be done early morning or late afternoon; avoid pouring onto a slab baking in full midday sun, because the surface dries too fast and crazes. / Check the forecast before starting outdoors — rain landing on an uncured pour washes material out and ruins the surface, so you want 6–8 hours clear of rain ahead of you.
