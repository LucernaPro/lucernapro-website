Solid Floor
One millimetre thin, and it stays put

Ordinary cement mortar already starts getting nervous below a centimetre. At one millimetre it will craze and lift off in sheets, every single time — because plain cement relies on its own thickness to hold itself together. Solid Floor is a polymer cement built specifically for this job. Trowel it from 1 to 5 mm. The finish is hard and dust-free, it does not chip away like ordinary render, it bonds to damp concrete as well as dry, and it has no smell — you can work inside the house with it.

And here is the honest part, before you spend anything: this material is difficult to trowel. You get 40 minutes from the moment it is mixed, and the look of the finished floor comes down almost entirely to the hand holding the trowel. Read the STRAIGHT TALK section before you decide.

Why Solid Floor

A concrete floor with a ruined surface leaves you two options: break it out and pour again, or skim over the top. The first costs a fortune and a week of your life. The second costs very little — except that ordinary cement cannot do it. That gap is the entire reason this product exists.

01

Genuinely 1 mm, without lifting

Plain cement mortar holds itself together with mass. Take the mass away and it crazes and delaminates. This one carries a polymer system inside the mix that does the holding instead of thickness, so it goes down anywhere from 1 to 5 mm — you resurface the whole floor without raising it into the bottom of your doors.

02

Hard and dust-free, for good

Test your existing floor in five seconds: rub your palm hard across it. If white powder comes off on your hand, the wearing surface is already finished. A floor like that sheds dust forever — sweep it today and it is back tomorrow. Skimming over it shuts off the source rather than chasing the symptom. The new surface is hard, washes down easily, and does not chip like ordinary render.

03

Damp concrete is not a problem

Almost every floor coating on the market loses to moisture still sitting in the slab — you put it down and the film blisters. This is a cement-based system, so it bonds well to damp substrates as well as dry ones, and it resists the alkalinity of concrete, which is the thing quietly destroying a lot of coatings from underneath.

04

The layer that makes floor paint last

When floor paint blisters and peels, the paint is usually not the culprit — the crumbling surface underneath is. The paint bonded faithfully to a layer of dust that was bonded to nothing at all. Skimming this on first, then painting, is you buying durability for the coating system you are about to pay for anyway.

We just won't pretend anybody can trowel it

40minutes. That is every minute you get, from the moment it is mixed

There are products on this site we happily describe as being for lazy people. This is not one of them. Solid Floor is a very good material that is hard to use, and we would rather you knew that now than at the moment it starts going off in the bucket in the middle of your living room.

The big one: it is hard to trowel

This is a trowel job, not a roller job. Flatness, even thickness, and the joins between one bay and the next all come out of the operator's hands and nowhere else. Somebody who has never rendered before will get a surface with trowel marks, waves, and thick and thin patches. Nothing is wrong with the material — it is still hard and still dust-free — but it will not look like the photograph. If you are doing this yourself, start in a small corner nobody looks at and let your hands meet the material once before it matters.

40 minutes is a deadline, not a suggestion

Once mixed you must have it down inside 40 minutes. After that it begins to set, and forcing it down anyway gives you a layer that never really bonded. There is exactly one solution: mix only what you can lay. Divide the floor into small bays and mix a fresh bucket for each one. Mixing a big batch to save trips is precisely how people end up pouring the whole thing away — nobody has ever lost face for mixing too little.

One splash of extra water and it is over

The mix takes 5–7 kg of water per set. Halfway through laying, your hands will want more, because more water spreads easier. Don't. Extra water slows the set down and takes strength out of the finished surface. If it is dragging, fix it with your timing and your trowel angle, not with the hose.

It is not a self-levelling screed

Slopes, dishes that hold water, long slow waves in the slab — this material does not flow across and fill them for you. The level you end up with is the level your hand puts there. If you need a genuinely flat plane, that is a different job and it has to be solved in the layer below.

What is underneath has to be sound and still

A new skin of 1–5 mm cannot hold together a slab that is settling or moving. A live crack will work its way up through the new layer eventually. Fill cracks and holes properly first with PatchPro. And freshly poured concrete must cure for at least 4 weeks, with moisture below 10%, before anything goes on it.

Every job needs the surface opened up. No shortcuts

Old concrete carries dust, oil, and cured-in release and curing agents that you cannot see but which will happily stop anything from bonding. It has to be ground or sanded open first, then vacuumed properly. This step eats more time than the rest of the job put together, and it is the one everybody wants to skip.

How to use it — all on this page

No separate manual to go hunting for. The real ratios and timings are all here — read it through before you open the first bag, because this job gives you no time to come back and read halfway.

  1. Check the slab first — new concrete has to be cured

    Newly poured concrete or screed must cure for at least 4 weeks, and moisture content must be below 10%, before you skim it. Not there yet? Then don't start. On an old floor, rub your palm hard across it: white powder on your hand means the wearing surface has gone, which is exactly the case this product was built for — but the loose material still has to be ground back to something solid first. new slab: 4 weeksmoisture ≤ 10%

  2. Get every contaminant off, properly

    The surface has to be clean, sound and dry, free of dust, oil, grease and salt deposits, and anything else sitting on it. The one everybody forgets: form release agents and curing compounds — invisible to the eye, and a perfect barrier against bonding. They have to come off. no oil or greasewash off saltsremove curing compound

  3. Grind it open, then vacuum it clean

    Open the surface with a floor grinder or suitable abrasive paper so the pores of the concrete are exposed and the new material has something to key into. This step decides whether the job stays down or lifts. It cannot be skipped. Grinding always creates a fresh batch of dust of its own — vacuum it off completely before you skim, not a token sweep.

  4. Fill cracks and holes first

    Cracks and deep pits must be filled flush and left to cure before you skim. Skim straight over a live crack and it will find its way back up through the new surface. Use PatchPro for floor repairs and let it cure fully as stated on that product.

  5. Mix in this order. Do not change it

    Ratio: A : B : C = 3 : 1.5 : 20 by weight

    1) Blend Part A and Part B together first until completely uniform · 2) add 5–7 kg of water and mix in · 3) only then add Part C (the powder) and mix for no more than 30 seconds, just until it is one consistent material.

    Never put the powder in first, and never keep mixing the powder longer than you need to — carrying on whips air into the mix and pushes the set along faster. The moment it looks uniform, stop. Mix only what you can lay inside 40 minutes. A : B : C = 3 : 1.5 : 20water 5–7 kgpowder: 30 seconds maxmix small batches

  6. Trowel it straight away — 1 to 5 mm

    Once mixed, get it onto the floor immediately with a steel trowel. Pick the thickness to suit the job: 1 mm to resurface only · 2–5 mm where you also need to take up level or fill shallow pitting. Set out small bays and decide your working direction before you begin, so you are never walking backwards over your own work. steel trowel1–5 mmlay it all within 40 min

  7. The waiting times, in full

    Touch dry: within 4 hours · Walk on: within 12 hours · Recoat or skim again: within 12 hours · In service: 2 days · Fully hard: 7 days

    For the first 7 days keep heavy things from being dragged across it, avoid aggressive scrubbing, and don't let water stand on it. touch dry ≤ 4 hwalk on ≤ 12 hin service 2 daysfully hard 7 days

  8. Painting over it? Wait until it is fully hard

    The finished surface is ready to take paint without being re-abraded, but it must be fully cured and genuinely dry first. General floors: ThermaGlaze · factories with chemicals or forklift traffic: Epoxy-PU floor coating · or leave it as bare cement, which is a perfectly reasonable answer here, because on its own it does not dust.

  9. Wash tools with water, immediately

    It is a cement system, so plain water does the job while it is still wet. Once it has set, getting it off means chipping, and that is the end of that trowel. Don't leave the bucket and tools standing while you finish up and deal with them at the end of the day.

The mistakes that waste the most material — never exceed 7 kg of water per set, it slows the set and costs you strength / never mix more than you can lay in 40 minutes / never skim onto a surface that has not been ground open / never skim over cracks that have not been repaired / never skim onto new concrete that has not had its 4 weeks
Storage — keeps for up to 1 year in unopened containers, stored below 37°C. Which means not in a vehicle and not under a metal roof that bakes all day.

Where it earns its money

This product wins outright on one specific job: a floor whose surface has failed across the board while the structure underneath is still perfectly good. Breaking that out is overkill. Leaving it alone means dust forever.

Break it out, or skim over it

On a concrete floor that has been in service for years, the wearing surface always fails long before the structure does — shallow spalling, running cracks, and dust coming off it permanently — while the slab underneath is still carrying load without complaint. Breaking that out means paying for demolition, disposal, fresh concrete and a week of downtime, all to fix a problem living in the top few millimetres.

Solid Floor exists for exactly that gap: a thin skim that gives you a new surface, harder than the old one and free of dust, with the floor level barely moving. The doors still shut. The thresholds stay where they are.

The floor level barely moves

This is the advantage people overlook most often. Most resurfacing options add several centimetres and end with you planing doors and rebuilding thresholds. One millimetre means everything else in the room stays exactly where it was.

A surface that is ready for paint

If painting the floor was the plan anyway, skimming first is you buying durability for the coating you are about to apply — because the paint gets to bond to something dense and even, rather than to a layer of powder that is waiting to lift off and take your paint with it.
