Why OffShore

Wood coatings mostly split into two camps — the ones that smell so strong you have to leave the house, and the cheap waterborne ones that turn yellow within a few months. This one exists in the gap nobody was occupying.

01

Open it and use it. There is nothing to get wrong.

Single component. No mixing, no weighing, no clock running. There is no pot life chasing you around the room, and whatever you don't use goes back in the tub with the lid on and waits for next weekend. · Two-part coatings genuinely are tougher, but you pay for that with the rule that once it is mixed it must be used, and you throw money away every time you misjudge the quantity. Most joinery in a house should not have to make that trade.

02

Waterborne. No harsh smell. Work indoors.

Coat the wardrobe in the bedroom in the evening and sleep in that room the same night — that is the difference you notice immediately against a solvent wood coating that has you leaving the windows open all night. · Wash brushes and rollers in plain water. No thinner to buy just to clean up afterwards, and no pile of thinner-soaked rags sitting in the house to worry about.

03

The film does not yellow — wood stays the colour of wood

This is exactly where cheap wood coatings lose. · Plenty of ordinary coatings go progressively yellower with age and light, and on pale timber like pine or rubberwood you get an orange-brown cast within months. The cruellest part is that it shows worst where something has been sitting on the surface, because that patch yellows more slowly than everything around it — and you are left with a permanent shadow of a fruit bowl. · This one holds the original colour of the timber. Pale wood stays pale, dark wood stays as dark as it is supposed to be.

04

High adhesion, and flexible enough to move with the wood

Wood is not a material that sits still — it expands and contracts with the humidity all year round. A film that is too hard crazes and then lifts off in sheets as the timber moves underneath it. · This film is flexible enough to move with the wood, so it resists cracking and peeling, and it handles everyday scuffing and moisture well.

What is it made of — a German-grade waterborne polymer system built specifically for timber. · We do not publish raw material names or formulations, as a matter of company policy. We will happily answer any question about what it can and cannot do, which is the part that actually affects your job.
STRAIGHT TALK — read this before you pay

The film survives water.
Your timber does not.

7days of continuous soak testing the film passed — the timber won't

Wood coatings are usually advertised as "waterproof" and then everyone is left to interpret that however they like — which is how people end up putting one on a balcony deck that ponds after rain and complaining afterwards. So let us draw the line clearly: a film that survives water is not the same thing as timber that should live in it.

The big one: don't use it under water — even though the film passed the test

We soaked this film in water continuously for a week and it held. But that was a test of the film — not a licence to sink your timber. · The thing that fails first is not the coating, it is the wood: timber that sits in standing water pulls in moisture from every direction and swells, warps, and moves — and once the wood moves that hard, no film on earth keeps up with it. · Water spilled and wiped up, rain blown against it and then dried — no problem at all. But balcony decks that hold puddles, timber that is actually submerged, the rim of a basin that is wet all day, the wet zone of a bathroom — none of that is this product's job. Don't use it there, no matter how many coats you put on.

Mainly interior work

Where it performs best is timber under a roof — furniture, wardrobes, shelving, doors, frames, staircases, panelling, timber ceilings. · Exterior work is possible, but restrict it to vertical surfaces that don't take water directly, such as a door under an eave or panelling with a roof over it. · For timber that stands in full sun and rain all year, this will coat it, but you have to accept that you will be recoating on a schedule. No clear coating on earth goes on once and wins an argument with the Thai sun.

Not recommended for timber floors people walk on

A real timber floor is a uniquely brutal place — bodyweight, plus the grit under everyone's shoes doing the work of sandpaper every day. · This film handles normal scuffing well, but we do not recommend it for a wood floor with daily foot traffic, because it will wear a visible path across the room long before you are happy about it. · For floors that get walked on, ask us first. We will point you at the right system even when it isn't this one.

It is a film, not a stain — and it is not invisible

Once it goes on, the timber gets darker and richer, the way wood looks when the surface is wet. That is normal for every clear coating in the world and it is not a defect, but it is not what everybody wants. If you want the wood to look exactly like bare sanded timber, this is not your product. · How to see it before you commit: put a drop of water on the sanded wood. The colour you see while it is still wet is the colour you are going to get.

It does not fill chips and splits for you

A clear coat is a thin film that follows the shape of whatever is underneath it. Chips, nail holes, a gouge out of a rebate — coat over them and every one is still there, arguably more obvious than before, because everything around them is now smooth. · Fill and sand flat first, then coat.

One coat is not enough, and never add water

One coat leaves a film too thin to resist anything meaningful — always do two, and the 5 sqm per kilo figure we quote already assumes two. · And never thin it with water to stretch it out. Waterborne does not mean you may add water. The moment you do, the film gets thinner, adhesion drops, and the timber you have to redo costs a great deal more than the coating you saved.

How to use it — all of it, on this page

No separate manual to go and find. Grit numbers and real waiting times are all here — read to the end before you open the first tub.

  1. Check the timber first — it has to be genuinely dry

    The wood must be dry, clean, and free of dust, grease and moisture. · Timber that has just been rained on, just been wiped down, or stored somewhere damp needs to sit in a shaded, ventilated spot until it is properly dry — coating over moisture trapped in the timber is simply sealing it in, and the film will go cloudy or blister later. Timber must be dryNo grease

  2. Old timber with an existing finish must be stripped back

    Wood that has previously had lacquer, shellac, another clear finish, penetrating oil or paint on it must be stripped or sanded back to bare timber first. Coating over something that is already on its way off is just laying a new film onto something that is about to let go — and it will take yours with it. · Previously oiled or waxed timber is the hardest case of all, because the oil is already inside the wood. If you're unsure, test a small corner first.

  3. Fill chips and splits first

    Chips, nail holes and missing sections must be filled flush and sanded flat before coating, because a clear coat will not hide them. · FillerAce works on timber — let it cure fully as stated on that product before you sand.

  4. Sand with 240–320 grit, along the grain

    Sand the surface smooth with 240–320 grit to open it up for adhesion. · Always sand along the grain, never across it — cross-grain scratches jump out the instant the coating goes on. · When you're done, get the dust off completely with a damp cloth or air, and let it dry before coating. 240–320 gritSand along the grainRemove all dust

  5. Stir it in — don't shake it

    Stir slowly and thoroughly, lifting from the bottom of the tub, before every session. Some settling during storage is normal for a waterborne product. · Do not shake the tub, because that beats air into the material and you get fine bubbles trapped in the cured film — and if you have already made bubbles, stand it aside and let them collapse before you start.

  6. First coat — thin and even

    Brush, roller or spray, whichever suits. · Go on thin and even, following the grain. Don't lay it on thick hoping to finish in one pass — a heavy film doesn't cure through, the surface wrinkles, and it dries far slower than it should. · Plan the work so you finish a whole face at a time rather than stopping mid-panel and coming back, or you'll see the join. Brush / roller / sprayThin, along the grain

  7. Wait 2–4 hours, then de-nib before the second coat

    Once the first coat is dry, run your hand over it and the surface will feel rougher than the bare wood did — nothing has gone wrong. The grain raises when it meets water, and it happens with every waterborne coating on the market. · The fix is to sand very lightly with 320–400 grit until it feels smooth again (you're knocking off raised fibres, not cutting through the film), then remove the dust completely. · This is the step that decides whether the job ends up silky or rough, and it is far and away the most commonly skipped one. Wait 2–4 hrs320–400 grit, lightlyDust off again

  8. Second coat to finish

    Same again — thin and even. · Two coats is the standard the coverage figures in the price table are based on. · For surfaces that take a real beating, such as a desktop or a door edge that gets handled daily, a third coat is worth doing, de-nibbing lightly between coats as before.

  9. Waiting times — the real numbers

    Touch dry 30–60 minutes · Recoat after 2–4 hours · Light use after 24 hours · Fully hard after 7 days

    Those first seven days are the window where the film is still building strength — avoid leaving heavy objects standing on it, dragging things across it, and letting water sit. · A cold glass left on a table you coated yesterday is the single most common casualty. Touch dry 30–60 minRecoat 2–4 hrsLight use 24 hrsFully hard 7 days

  10. Wash the tools in plain water, straight away

    It's waterborne — wash brushes and rollers in plain water while the material is still wet. No thinner needed. · Once it has dried in a brush it is extremely difficult to get out, so don't put the brush down and plan to deal with it in the evening. · Close the lid firmly every time; what's left keeps for the next job.

The mistakes that waste the most product — never thin it with water to stretch it / never coat timber that is still damp / never lay it on thick hoping to finish in one coat / never coat over lacquer or old paint that hasn't been stripped / never use it where water stands, or on submerged timber
Storage — keep it somewhere dry, between 5 and 35°C, out of direct sunlight, and keep the lid firmly closed. · Don't leave it in a vehicle or under a metal roof that bakes all day. · Apply at normal room temperature with air moving through — very humid weather or a full day of rain will make it dry a great deal slower than the numbers above.

Where it earns its money

Where this one wins outright is interior timber you want to stay looking like timber — tougher, easier to wipe down, and otherwise unchanged.

Slab tops, single-piece boards and anything where the grain is the whole point

If you paid for a beautiful slab to make a table out of, the entire reason you paid for it is that piece of grain — so coating it with something that will be yellow in six months is a slow way of erasing the thing you bought.

This is the arena where a non-yellowing film shows a difference you can actually see years later, and it's why furniture makers and interior fit-out crews are the group that reorders this one most often.

Old timber, reclaimed boards, coarse open grain

Weathered timber with an open, fibrous surface suits this product well, because a porous surface gives it a great deal to hold onto. · Just budget for the fact that the first coat will disappear into it far faster than into a dense surface. If it looks under-coated, put it right on the second pass — don't keep going back over the same spot while the first coat is still wet.

Work that has to live with people all day

Wardrobes in bedrooms, shelving in a child's room, a desk — the biggest advantage of a waterborne product here isn't technical at all, it's that you finish coating and then stay in the room. No hauling the furniture outside to work on it and hauling it back in again.
