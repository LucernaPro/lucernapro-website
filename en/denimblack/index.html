1×THIN COAT · light passes win

DenimBlack's pigment is translucent — and that's exactly why the result looks real. The color deepens along the fabric's own weave instead of burying everything under one opaque sheet. If your goal is solid pitch black, automotive spray paint does that cheaper — and your fabric will turn stiff as cardboard and look fake instantly. We chose the realistic route.

The trade-off, said plainly: thin coats only. Heavily faded fabric needs managed expectations — multiple passes use more product, and the thicker the pigment builds, the stiffer the fabric gets. And this isn't a permanent-forever dye: wear it, wash it, and the color gradually fades like any dyed fabric. When it does, just spray again — one can lasts several rounds.

Prove the durability yourself

Spray it on black fabric and how would you ever know it holds? Spray a scrap of white fabric instead — wash it however you like, it is never coming back white. That's the grip level of this pigment.

Water-repellent coated fabric = no deal

Fabric with a waterproof coating won't take the pigment. Full stop. And on every fabric, test a small hidden spot first — inside hem, for example — before committing to the whole piece.

Washing it to last

Every dye on earth bleeds with washing, ours included — wash gently and keep it away from whites for the first few washes and the color stays with you far longer.

How to use

Everything you need is on this page — the two keys are clean, bone-dry fabric and thin, even passes. Nail both and the finish comes out clean every time.

Test before you buy: will spray work on your fabric?

Ten seconds, no tools — drop some water on the fabric and watch where it goes.

✓ Water soaks in = good to go

If the drop sinks into the weave, the fabric is open to liquids — the dye can soak in and bond with the fibres the same way. You're clear to spray.

✗ Water beads up and rolls off = won't work

The fabric left the factory with a water-repellent coating — if water can't get in, neither can the dye. It will sit on the surface and flake off fast. Stop here, save your money.

  1. Prep the fabric — clean and fully dry

    Wash the garment and let it dry completely first — dust, oils or leftover moisture will block the pigment from gripping the fibers properly.

  2. Always test a hidden spot first

    Spray a small unseen area like the inside hem, let it dry, check the result — then commit to the full piece.

    Test every time

  3. Set up — mask what shouldn't get hit

    Lay the piece flat, put paper or cloth under and around it — parts you don't want colored like buttons, zippers and leather patches, cover with masking tape.

  4. Shake the can well

    Shake hard for about 1 minute so the pigment mixes through — during longer jobs, shake again periodically.

    Shake ~1 min

  5. Spray thin, even passes over the whole piece

    Hold the can 15–20 cm from the fabric, sweep in continuous strokes, light and even — never soak one spot or the pigment pools into patches. Finish one side, flip, do the other.

    15–20 cm awayThin & even

  6. Let it dry — repeat if very faded, but be honest with yourself

    Give it 30–60 minutes to surface-dry, then judge — heavily faded fabric can take another light coat, but straight talk: every extra coat costs more product and builds pigment that stiffens the fabric. Stop when the color satisfies you. Don't get greedy.

    Surface-dry 30–60 minThin wins

  7. Finish — two paths depending on the item

    Caps, bags, backpacks: water-based formula — once fully dry, grab it and go. / Clothes you wear: let it cure fully for 24 hours, then run one first wash before wearing — gentle cycle, away from whites — and it's ready for the street.

    Caps/bags: dry & goClothes: first wash before wearing
⚠ Safety: Spray in a well-ventilated area, avoid inhaling the mist directly, keep out of reach of children.
Inside the can: pressurized with nitrogen (N₂) — non-flammable, non-explosive, unlike common flammable-propellant sprays. A lot more peace of mind for indoor use.
