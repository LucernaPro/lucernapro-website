DeepStick on ABS Pool Wall Seams — One of the Hardest Joints We've Filled

Published Aug 2026 · by the LucernaPro team

This job was a pool built with an ABS wall panel system — prefabricated plastic panels — which turns out to be one of the toughest surfaces in the business to find a good joint filler for. It's a smooth, glossy plastic with no pores for anything to grip onto mechanically, unlike concrete or tile.

The seams between panels run vertically the full height of the wall, at every point where two panels meet. Water getting behind them has to be stopped completely, but the seal also has to survive the panels shifting with water pressure and temperature, day after day, without cracking.

01The ABS Wall Seams — the Hardest Part of This Job

Each ABS panel meets the next one, and every meeting point becomes a vertical seam. If that seam isn't sealed completely, water works its way in behind the panel where you can't see it from the front — by the time it shows, it's usually already spread.

Whatever fills that seam has to do three things at once: bond to a glossy ABS plastic surface for real, flex with a seam that keeps moving without tearing, and hold up to permanent water immersion for the life of the pool.

02Why Ordinary Fillers Can't Handle This Surface

Cement or a standard hard resin adhesive won't bond well to ABS — the surface is too smooth, with nothing for it to grip mechanically, so it eventually lets go along the seam.

Ordinary rubber caulks and sealants flex fine, but plenty of them were never built for permanent submersion. The material degrades, swells, or shrinks over a few years, opening up a gap for water to get through again.

The same job also had hairline cracks in the pool's concrete floor that needed the exact same standard — permanent water resistance and real adhesion, not just surface contact waiting to fail.

03DeepStick Closed Both the Wall and the Floor on the Same Job

DeepStick runs on HydroCure Polymer technology powered by Covestro, which gives it strong adhesion even on an unusual surface like ABS. It stays flexible after it's fully cured, so the seam can keep moving with water pressure and temperature without tearing, and it holds up to permanent submersion for real.

The crew ran it in one continuous vertical pass down the full depth of each seam — the same technique used on every job — to avoid trapping air voids, which are the number one reason a sealed joint starts leaking again later.

The result: every ABS wall seam sealed solid top to bottom, and the concrete floor's hairline cracks closed in the same pass with the same material — no switching between products for different surfaces on the same job.
