Waterproofing From the Inside — The “Wrong” Fix That's Sometimes Your Only Option

Published Aug 2026 · by the LucernaPro team

Let's be blunt from the very first line: fixing a water leak from inside the house is, by the book, the wrong way to do it. That's not a sales hook — it's basic building engineering that every waterproofing shop should tell you before selling you anything.

But this article exists because real life is full of jobsites like the photo below — a bedroom wall shared with the house next door, moisture pushing through from the other side, paint blistering, render flaking off in sheets, white salt stains blooming along the lower wall. You can't exactly climb over the fence and coat your neighbour's wall.

01There's only one correct rule — seal the side the water comes from

Proper waterproofing (the Positive Side) means coating the face the water actually hits. Whichever side the rain drives against, that's the side you seal, so water never enters the masonry in the first place. Physics is entirely on your side here: water pressure presses the film against the surface, so the harder the water pushes, the tighter the film seals.

Coating from the inside (the Negative Side) means standing in the way of water that is already inside the masonry. Now that same pressure becomes your enemy — it pushes on the film from behind, around the clock, trying to shove it off the wall. This is why ordinary waterproofers designed for exterior use die in this arena by the truckload: they blister, they peel, and the water comes straight back.

02But real life has jobsites where you can't get outside

Townhomes with party walls butted against the neighbour's. Condos where the wall legally belongs to the building. Shophouses where the far side of your wall is someone else's home. Basements where the far side is soil. These cases exist everywhere, and they all share one thing: the side the water comes from is not a side you can reach.

The photo at the top of this article is a textbook example. Moisture travels through the masonry from the neighbour's side and evaporates out through the interior face of the wall, carrying dissolved mineral salts with it. Those salts crystallise on the surface as white stains (efflorescence), pushing the paint into blisters and peeling it off in sheets. Repaint it as many times as you like — it comes back, because house paint was never designed to resist water pushing from behind.

For cases like this — cases with genuinely no alternative — the industry developed dedicated negative-side waterproofing systems. And let's be honest about it up front: this is a harder fight than normal, because you're battling water that's already in, not keeping it out.

03What a system needs to survive the negative side

First, adhesion strong enough to fight the back-pressure (negative hydrostatic pressure). A film that merely sits on the surface gets kicked off within months. A system that survives has to soak into the concrete's pore structure and anchor itself as part of the substrate — not perch on top of it.

Second, surface preparation — and this is where most jobs fail, not the product. Old paint, loose render and surface grime must be ground back to bare concrete to open the pores so the liquid can penetrate deep. Skip this one step and the best product in the world becomes a thin surface coat, waiting its turn to be pushed off.

Third, cracks must be dealt with first, always. A crack is a highway for water, and coating systems were never designed to bridge large cracks. Cut them open, fill them flush with a repair compound like FillerAce, let it cure, and only then coat the waterproofing system over the top.

05One more time before you go — if you can reach the outside, go outside

DeepSeal and every negative-side system exist only for people who genuinely cannot do it the correct way. If your wall can still be coated from the outside, always coat it from the outside — it's more correct, it lasts longer, and it's usually cheaper too.
