Why IronLock

There is no shortage of anti-rust paint on the market, and most of it shares the same two weaknesses: solvent fumes strong enough that nobody can work nearby, and it will not stick to galvanised steel. This product exists to fix exactly those two things.

STRAIGHT TALK — read this before you open the tub

Painting anti-rust paint over rust buys you time.
It does not buy you protection.

6×ROUGHLY THE VOLUME RUST OCCUPIES VS THE STEEL IT ATE

The question we get more than any other is "I can't be bothered sanding the rust off — can I just paint over it?" The honest answer is yes, you can, and it will fail, and when it fails you will blame the paint even though the paint was never the problem. So let us explain it properly before you spend the money.

Rust is not dirt sitting on top of steel. It is steel that has already changed into something else. When iron meets water — humidity in the air is plenty — and oxygen, an electrochemical reaction starts on the surface: iron dissolves off as ions and combines with oxygen to form iron oxide. Add salt to the water — sea air, road dust, or just the sweat off someone's hand — and the water conducts better and the reaction runs faster.

And the iron oxide you end up with has two properties that ruin everything: it takes up several times more room than the steel it consumed, and it is not firmly attached to the parent steel underneath. That is exactly why rust flakes off by itself without anyone picking at it.

Paint grips rust. Rust does not grip steel.

Paint over rust and the paint bonds to the rust beautifully. The problem is that the weakest layer in the system is the joint between the rust and the steel. When it lets go, it lets go as one piece — paint and rust together — leaving you looking at bare steel.

Sealing it in traps the moisture and the salt

Rust is porous and it holds moisture and salt inside those pores. Seal a paint film over the top and you have not stopped the reaction — you have simply moved it somewhere you cannot see. By the time you notice, the paint is already blistering.

Rust expands, and it cracks the paint from underneath

The reaction carrying on below keeps making new rust, and that new material needs several times the room the old steel occupied. That pressure is what lifts and splits the paint film — not poor paint quality.

Going on thicker does not help

A heavier coat only makes it take longer to show. It does not stop it — and when it does show, the damage is worse, because the rust underneath had longer to work. You lose the paint, the time and the labour twice.

There is rust already — strip it or convert it

Two routes exist: take the rust back to bare steel, or turn the rust into something stable that is bonded to the steel. There is no third route. Find your situation below.

New steel, new galvanised, new roofing sheet — no rust yet→ Degrease it properly, then two coats of IronLock straight on
Light rust — spots or a brown bloom, nothing flaking yet→ Convert it with Revival first, then IronLock over the top
Heavy rust — scale you can lift off in sheets→ Knock or grind the loose scale off first, then Revival
Structural steel, warehouses, roof frames where sanding it all is simply not realistic→ This is the job Revival was designed for
How Revival works — it is a water-based rust converter with a mild acid in it. Brushed onto rust, the acid reacts with the crumbly iron oxide and turns it into a stable, water-insoluble iron compound that is anchored to the steel underneath, leaving a sealed film behind. The result is a surface that is "dead" — something IronLock can grip, instead of gripping flakes that are waiting to fall off. — See Revival →
Never apply Revival over painted areas — there is a mild acid in it; scrape the paint off the rusted area first. And where the rust is heavy and flaking, the loose material always has to come off first. Do not expect the liquid to do the wire brush's whole job — a rust converter means less sanding, not no sanding.

How to use it — everything is on this page

No separate manual to hunt down. The real numbers are all here.

  1. Work out what the surface actually is

    Rust or no rust. If there is rust, go back up the page and deal with it first — this step cannot be skipped. Everything below depends on it.

  2. Get the oil and dust off completely

    New steel, new galvanised and roofing sheet almost always arrive with a thin mill oil or anti-corrosion film from the factory — invisible, but real, and the single most common reason a coating peels when everything else was done right. Clean it with a degreaser or soapy water, rinse, and let it dry completely before you start. Surface must be dryNo oilNo dust

  3. Stir it properly

    The solids settle during storage. Stir from the bottom of the tub until the colour and consistency are uniform — not two swirls across the top. Stir it badly and the anti-corrosive pigment stays in the bottom of the tub while you brush the liquid above it onto the steel. Stir before every use

  4. First coat — no primer underneath

    Brush, roller or spray. Thin and even beats one heavy coat, especially on round sections, corners, welds and fastener heads — the places where the film thins out, and, as it happens, exactly where rust starts first. No primer neededThin, even coats

  5. Wait 30–60 minutes, then the second coat

    Drying time depends on the weather — humid or cool days take longer. Wait until it is dry to the touch, then apply the second coat across the direction of the first, so you are not left with brush lines where the film is thinner than everywhere else. Two coats is standardSecond coat crosswise

  6. Wash the tools immediately, with water

    The upside of a water-based system — plain water while the paint is still wet. Once it dries, water will not touch it. Do not leave the brushes to deal with at the end of the day.

  7. Go easy on it for the first 24 hours

    A water-based film sets quickly, but full hardness comes later — keep water, abrasion and heavy loads off it for the first 24 hours. After that, normal service.

The mistakes that waste the most product — never thin it with water to stretch it out; a thinner film throws away the protection you paid for. Do not paint if rain is due within a few hours — a water-based coating that has not dried yet will simply run off. Do not paint onto wet or dew-covered surfaces. And avoid roofing sheet in full midday sun when the panel is baking hot — the film dries too fast to flow out evenly. Early morning or late afternoon is better.

Where it pays for itself

A few hundred baht of anti-rust paint extending the life of something that cost thousands — that is where this product earns its money, not where it looks nice.

Rainwater gutters — coat the whole run, inside and out

A gutter is the piece of galvanised steel that takes more water than anything else on the house: rain straight onto it, water standing in it after the rain stops, and rotting leaf litter holding moisture against it all year round — and galvanising is nowhere near as tough as people assume. The zinc layer is thin, and once it has worn through, the steel underneath goes fast.

So do not just coat the outside to make it look tidy — coat the entire run, the outer face and the inside of the channel. This product handles standing water without complaint; coat the inside and let the water run over it.

Not just rust protection — erosion protection too

A year of water running through, carrying grit, dust and leaf debris, wears the gutter surface away long before rust ever appears. The IronLock film takes that wear instead of the metal, and when it thins out you simply recoat — considerably cheaper than replacing gutters.

The arithmetic that makes the decision easy

Once rust has eaten through, there is nothing to repair — the whole run comes down and gets replaced. New gutters plus installation labour costs many times more than one tub of anti-rust paint. Coating it on day one is the smallest amount of money you can spend to extend the life of something far more expensive.
