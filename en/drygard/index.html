What actually makes it different

The difference is in what the film is, not how thick it goes on — we are not selling you the thickest paint on the shelf, we are selling the one that survives on a damp wall

01

The waterproofing is in the paint, not in the advert

Ordinary house paint handles rain running down a dry wall and not much more. Once damp builds up inside the wall itself, it pushes the film until it blisters and lifts off in sheets. / DryGard's film is built to take damp head-on — water gets into the wall far less easily, and the film does not blister and peel the way ordinary paint does. That is the whole product, honestly stated.

02

Flexible, so it doesn't craze along with the wall

Walls move constantly — expanding in the heat, contracting at night. A brittle paint film eventually crazes right along with them. / DryGard's film is elastomeric: it stretches and relaxes with the wall without splitting, which is why it lasts longer on an elevation that takes sun and rain in turns all year. / But to be clear: "doesn't crack itself" is a completely different claim from "covers the cracks you already have." See STRAIGHT TALK point 02.

03

Open and paint — no mixing, no primer

No catalyst, no Part A and Part B, no thinning. Stir the pail through and start painting. / And no primer on normal work — it grips render, plaster, brickwork and metal-sheet roofing on its own. / Existing paint still well stuck? Paint straight over it. No stripping the whole house first.

04

Mildew resistant, wipes clean, reflects some heat

The tight film surface means marks cling less and wipe off more easily than on a flat matt paint, and there is a mildew inhibitor in the formula for hot, humid climates. / There are also heat-reflective solids in the paint, which take some heat off the wall surface — but if cutting heat is your actual goal, read STRAIGHT TALK point 05 first. We will tell you straight which product you really want.

STRAIGHT TALK — the bits paint companies skip

This product is genuinely good at its job. It is also the wrong product for several jobs people try to use it for. Read to the end before you order, so you don't waste your money.

01 · It's house paint, not floor paint

DryGard is made for walls and roofs — surfaces nobody walks on. / A flexible film like this does not take abrasion. Put it on a floor with foot or vehicle traffic and it will scuff into traffic lanes and go dark holding dirt. / Floors are an entirely different product. See Epoxy-PU floor coating.

02 · Don't buy it hoping it will cover your cracks

This is the point we most want to be clear about on this page — the film simply is not that thick. It is paint, not filler. / Very fine crazing, the sort you cannot catch a fingernail in, a flexible film will bridge to some degree. That is genuinely the limit of it. / Anything you can see as a distinct line, or get a fingernail into, has to be properly filled first — paint straight over a crack and it will come back through looking like you never touched it. / Wall cracks take FillerAce, and hairline cracks have to be ground open first before filling. Smeared over the surface, it will not hold.

03 · Paint that is already letting go will take this with it

DryGard grips harder than your old paint — but it can only grip what it is put on. / If what is underneath is old paint already on its way off the wall, one day the whole lot comes away together. / Anywhere that sounds hollow or lifts when you scrape it has to come off completely first. Anywhere still firmly stuck, paint over it and carry on.

04 · If the water is pushing from behind, painting the front won't fix it

Walls in contact with soil, basement walls, walls drawing damp up from the ground, or groundwater pushing in from the other side — that is negative-side water pressure, and it will eventually push any paint on the market off the wall. This one included. / That job has to be dealt with inside the wall first using DeepSeal, and painted afterwards.

05 · It reflects heat, but that is not what it is for

There genuinely are heat-reflective solids in the paint and they genuinely help a little — but the shade you pick matters enormously here. The darker the shade, the more of that reflectance you throw away. / If your real objective is getting the heat under a roof down in a way you can feel, the product built for that is HeatShield — white, with reflectance figures from an actual testing institute. DryGard is waterproofing paint that happens to reflect some heat, not the other way round.

06 · If your walls aren't damp, ordinary paint is fine

Per square metre this still costs more than ordinary house paint. / If your walls are dry, have never blistered and have never peeled, and you simply fancy a change of colour — go and buy normal house paint. We sell to people with a problem. We don't pretend everybody has one.

Want the waterproofing without changing the colour of your house? If your walls already look how you want them and you just need a water-resistant layer over the top, the product for that is AquaShell — clear, goes over existing paint, changes nothing about how the house looks.

How to use it

Every real number is on this page. You will not need to go and find a manual anywhere else.

Check the wall first — dry, clean, sound

The wall must be fully dry, free of dust, free of white salt bloom, free of algae. / New render has to cure first — do not rush to paint over cement that is still holding moisture. / On previously painted walls: tap and scrape to find anything hollow or lifting, and take it all off. Whatever is still firmly stuck can stay and be painted over.

Fill the cracks properly first — this step is not optional

Do not expect the paint to stand in for a proper repair. Anything you can see as a line or get a fingernail into must be filled before you paint — use FillerAce. / Hairline cracks have to be ground open with a disc before filling; smeared over the surface they will not stay. / Awkward joints such as fibre-cement board or slick surfaces take DeepStick.

Permanently damp walls — prime first

On normal work you do not need a primer; DryGard goes straight on. / But on a wall that stays heavily damp more or less permanently — the elevation that takes the driving rain, walls near ground level, corners that never dry out — put one coat of Marine Guard down as a primer first and DryGard over it. That combination fixes the problem far more completely.

Open the pail, stir it right through — then paint

Nothing to mix. No Part A and Part B. No thinning. / Stir until the paint is genuinely uniform through the whole pail (the heavy solids settle out while it sits). / Never add water to stretch it. Thinned paint means a thinner film, which means throwing away exactly the water resistance you paid for. / Brush or roller, either is fine.

First coat — full and even

Lay the paint on full and even across the whole face, no thin patches and no heavy patches. / Work section by section and never let a wet edge dry off before you come back to it, or the join will show.

Wait 2 hours, then the second coat

Two hours between coats. / Run the second coat across the direction of the first (first coat rolled up and down, second coat side to side) — that closes every thin spot your eye cannot pick up. / Two coats is the system. One coat is not waterproofing, it is just expensive house paint.

Leave it 8–12 hours after the last coat

After 8–12 hours it is ready for service. / Keep water off it in the meantime, and do not lean anything against it or scrape it. / Do not paint if rain is coming. A film that has not set yet meeting driving rain means the whole coat is wasted.

Wash tools immediately, while the paint is still wet

Wash brushes, rollers and trays out with water straight away while the paint is still wet. / Let a film like this dry on your tools and it becomes extremely difficult to get off. Do not find out the hard way.

The timings, short version: two coats · 2 hours between coats · ready for service 8–12 hours after the last coat · 1 kg ≈ 5 sq.m over two coats · no mixing · no primer (except on permanently damp walls)
